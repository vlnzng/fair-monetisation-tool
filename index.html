Why this exists

Many game shops make buying easier than deciding. Prices hide behind currencies, offers run on countdowns, and children are asked to pay.

EU law covers much of this, but it is written for lawyers. This tool turns it into plain questions about what a player sees.

Method and limits

For anyone who wants to know how the result comes about.

How levels are worked out
  1. 1Each answer counts. Yes counts nothing, Unsure a little, No the most. Some questions weigh more than others.
  2. 2Under-18s raise the stakes. If young players are in the audience, answers about offers and protection count more.
  3. 3Every area gets a level. Minimal, Moderate or Elevated, based on the answers in that area.
  4. 4The overall level follows the areas. One Elevated area makes the result Elevated. Two Moderate areas make it at least Moderate. Many smaller points can add up as well.
  5. 5One answer decides on its own. An offer that tells children to buy, or to ask an adult, always sets Elevated.

Scope and limits

It looks at

  • Prices and virtual currencies
  • Confirming, cancelling and refunds
  • Pressure in offers
  • Protection of under-18s
  • Paid random items

It leaves out

  • Anything behind the interface, such as data or targeting
  • Game design beyond buying, such as engagement loops
  • Personalised prices
  • Whether a flow is legal

What changed in the tool
  • Plainer questions, each with an example
  • Three new questions: pay-to-win and paid random items
  • A first step that leaves out what your flow does not have
  • Every result line traces back to an answer
  • Four fixes to the scoring, documented in the repository
